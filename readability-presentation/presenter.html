UX Workshop

How software learned to fit humans.

A short history, then a live exercise.

01 · A short history

Humans used to bend to the machine.

  • The industrial revolution designed jobs around machines. The tool was the fixed variable.
  • Factory workers, sewing machines, typewriters — if you couldn't use it, that was a "you" problem.
  • Right through the early 20th century, designing the machine around the person wasn't really a thing.

02 · World War II

Then really good pilots started crashing perfectly good planes.

  • The Boeing B-17 Flying Fortress. One of the most important bombers of the war.
  • Fully functional aircraft, flown by the best-trained pilots in the world.
  • They'd land safely — then collapse the plane on the runway.
  • The Air Force wrote it off as pilot error.

03 · Alphonse Chapanis, 1943

It wasn't pilot error. It was design error.

  • Alphonse Chapanis, a psychologist in the Army Air Corps, went to look.
  • The flaps lever and the landing gear lever were shaped identically, mounted next to each other.
  • His fix: attach a rubber wheel to the gear lever and a triangular wedge to the flaps.
  • The "pilot errors" disappeared.
Before — two identical levers, side by side.
After — a wheel for gear, a plate for flaps.

04 · The field emerges

Fit the task to the person, not the person to the task.

  • The Air Force's aviation lab at Wright-Patterson kept studying it.
  • Paul Fitts, working in the same era, published Fitts's Law in 1954 — the time to reach a target depends on its distance and size.
  • The idea generalised out of aviation: cockpits, then telephones, then tractors, then office chairs.
  • The field got names: human factors in the US, ergonomics in the UK.

05 · The next shift

Then disability rights broadened the point.

  • Berkeley, 1970s. Ed Roberts and the Independent Living Movement fought for curb cuts: the small ramps at street corners.
  • Designed for wheelchair users. Used by everyone: strollers, delivery dollies, suitcases, cyclists.
  • Design for the edges, and the middle takes care of itself.
  • Ronald Mace, an architect who used a wheelchair, formalised this as Universal Design in 1985.

06 · Today

That's what UX is.

  • Every button, every form, every error message is a descendant of that shift.
  • The interfaces we build should still fit the person on the other side — under time pressure, on a screen reader, or on their fourteenth deal of the week.
  • Most "UX rules" are just formalised versions of what Chapanis worked out on that airfield.

07 · The exercise

Now you.

  • Open UX Quest — the link I sent you.
  • Eight scenarios. Short mini-apps with a usability flaw.
  • Everyone picks their answer, then we pause and discuss.
  • The point is the conversation, not the score.

Debrief · Question 1

A settings panel

Correct answer

Save each setting when it is toggled.

Match the control to the save model. Switches signal an immediate change. Checkboxes belong in a form that waits for Save. Mixing the two makes the state uncertain until commit.

Accessibility: screen readers announce switches with their current state. If the state isn't actually saved, the announcement lies. Switches must be immediately truthful.

Discuss

  • Where in our tools do we mix switches with Save flows?
  • Where would checkboxes plus a Save button actually be the better choice?

Debrief · Question 2

A new matter form

Correct answer

Keep 'Create matter' enabled and validate on submit.

Keep submit controls available. Disabled buttons hide the reason the user can't continue, and native disabled controls are skipped by keyboard navigation. Validate on submit, then place each error next to the field that failed.

Accessibility: disabled buttons are removed from the tab order. Hover tooltips don't fire for keyboard, touch, or screen-reader users. Two invisibility problems layered on top of each other.

Discuss

  • How often do we ship disabled submit buttons with no visible reason?
  • Validate on submit, on blur, or on change — where's the line?

Debrief · Question 3

A matter detail card

Correct answer

Edit the value inline, directly on the detail card.

Single-field edits belong inline. Modals earn their weight when the task is multi-step, destructive, or needs full focus. Editing one text field is none of those.

Accessibility: modals move focus, trap keyboard navigation, and cost users their sense of place. Inline editing keeps the action, the value, and the result in the same visual context.

Discuss

  • Which of our tools open a modal for a one-field edit?
  • Where does a modal genuinely earn its weight?

Debrief · Question 4

A sort menu

Correct answer

Reduce the menu to the sort orders people actually use.

Hick's Law: decision time grows with the number of options. Miller's Law: working memory holds about seven items. Both point at trimming and defaulting before adding structure.

Accessibility: long menus are exhausting under cognitive load. Users with ADHD, cognitive impairment, or reading in a second language benefit from short menus with clear defaults.

Discuss

  • What sort orders do you actually use day-to-day?
  • Where in our tools is a menu longer than it needs to be?

Debrief · Question 5

A risk score

Correct answer

Show the key factors inline near the score.

Put decision-critical context inline. Tooltips are for optional extras, not for the primary meaning of a number the user is about to act on.

Accessibility: hover tooltips don't exist for keyboard, touch, or screen-reader users. Explanations hidden inside them are invisible to a real slice of the audience.

Discuss

  • Where do we hide meaningful context behind an info icon?
  • What's the test for "tooltip vs inline"?

Debrief · Question 6

A document download

Correct answer

Disable the button on click and show that the download is in progress.

After a valid submit, show progress and block repeat activation. Fix the feedback in the interface; a server guard is only a safety net.

Accessibility: combine aria-busy="true", a visible spinner, and either a native disabled button or aria-disabled with a guarded handler. That covers screen-reader, sighted, and keyboard users at once.

Discuss

  • Have you ever double-clicked a submit because it looked frozen?
  • Where in our tools do we still have "silent" buttons?

Debrief · Question 7

A document summary

Correct answer

Label the summary and show what to verify.

Label AI output where it appears, and show what still needs human review. Provenance and review guidance belong inline, next to the content they describe.

Legal / professional angle: for lawyers, unverified AI shouldn't wear the same visual weight as a partner-reviewed analysis. That's not polish — it's a professional-responsibility question.

Discuss

  • How do we label AI output in our tools today?
  • Is the label loud enough that a partner scanning at 11pm won't miss it?

Debrief · Question 8

A filter sidebar

Correct answer

Update checks immediately and debounce the results request.

Update the control state immediately; debounce the request behind it. The interface should feel instant. The network doesn't have to be.

Accessibility: rapid re-renders on every keystroke or click can cause screen readers to re-announce content, disorienting users. Debouncing helps announcement stability.

Discuss

  • Where do we fire on every keystroke or toggle?
  • Sensible delays: search? filter? autosave?

Wrap-up

Two threads.

  • Feedback where the action is.
  • Explanations everyone can reach.
  • Almost every UX failure is a variation on one of these two.
  • Next time you're about to ship a disabled button with a hover tooltip, a corner toast, or an info icon hiding the point: remember the B-17.
